PARROTHIT Editorial Guidelines

Comprehensive standards for accurate, independent, fair and responsible journalism

Our commitment: PARROTHIT aims to publish journalism that is accurate, fair, independent, accountable and respectful of human dignity. These standards apply to news reports, features, interviews, photographs, video, headlines, explainers, opinion pieces, sponsored content and material submitted by contributors. They are editorial standards, not a claim of certification by any external body.

1. Accuracy, verification and evidence

  • Verify material facts before publication using reliable primary sources where practical, including official records, documents, direct observation and on-the-record interviews.
  • Seek independent corroboration for consequential claims. A viral post, anonymous message, screenshot or single unverified source is not sufficient proof on its own.
  • Check names, dates, locations, titles, figures, quotations, translations, image captions and context. Keep notes and records that allow editors to review the reporting.
  • Do not invent facts, sources, quotations, events, statistics, documents or eyewitness accounts. Do not present speculation as established fact.
  • When facts remain uncertain, explain what is known, what is not known and how the information was obtained. Update developing stories when verified information changes.

2. Independence, conflicts of interest and gifts

  • Editorial decisions must be based on news value and the public interest, not payment, political pressure, personal relationships, threats or favour.
  • Journalists and editors should disclose relevant financial, family, political, business or personal conflicts to their editor and recuse themselves when impartial reporting could reasonably be questioned.
  • Do not accept bribes, undisclosed payments, gifts or benefits in exchange for coverage, favourable treatment, silence or access. Any unavoidable hospitality must be modest, transparent and consistent with newsroom policy.
  • Advertisers, sponsors, donors and partners must not buy control over independent editorial conclusions.

3. Fairness, balance and the right of reply

  • Represent people and positions fairly and in context. Do not distort a statement by selectively quoting it or removing material context.
  • Give a person or organisation a reasonable opportunity to respond to serious allegations before publication when practicable. Record attempts to obtain a response and state fairly when no response was received.
  • Do not create false balance: give claims weight in proportion to the available evidence, not merely equal space.
  • Distinguish allegation, charge, investigation, conviction, acquittal and opinion accurately. Do not imply guilt before it is established by a competent process.
  • Use anonymous sources only when the information is important, the source has a credible basis for knowing it, the reason for anonymity is explained to an editor, and the identity is known to a responsible editor wherever possible.

4. Headlines, summaries and presentation

  • Headlines, thumbnails, push alerts, captions and social posts must accurately reflect the story and must not exaggerate or mislead to attract clicks.
  • Do not state as fact in a headline something the article only speculates about. Do not omit essential qualifications or use a photograph that creates a false impression.
  • Clearly label analysis, commentary, satire, reviews, sponsored articles and paid promotions. Opinion must not be disguised as straight news.

5. Corrections, updates and accountability

  • Correct significant factual errors promptly, clearly and proportionately. Do not quietly alter a material error in a way that misleads readers.
  • Where appropriate, mark a story “Correction,” “Update” or “Editor's note,” describe the material change and retain a transparent record of significant revisions.
  • For developing reports, show publication or update times when available and distinguish new verified facts from earlier reporting.
  • Review credible complaints in good faith. Explain editorial decisions respectfully and do not retaliate against people who raise genuine concerns.

6. Privacy, dignity and vulnerable people

  • Balance the public interest against potential harm. Public curiosity alone does not justify publishing private or intimate details.
  • Do not expose private addresses, personal phone numbers, identity documents, precise live locations or other sensitive personal data without a compelling and lawful public-interest reason.
  • Take special care with children, survivors of sexual violence, victims of abuse, grieving families, people in crisis and people who may not understand the consequences of publication. Do not identify child victims of sexual abuse.
  • Obtain informed consent where appropriate for sensitive interviews, images or recordings. Consent does not remove the newsroom's duty to consider foreseeable harm.
  • Avoid demeaning descriptions and stereotypes based on ethnicity, nationality, religion, disability, gender, age, health status or other personal characteristics unless a characteristic is genuinely relevant to the story.

7. Public safety, graphic material and emergencies

  • Do not publish material that needlessly glorifies violence, humiliates victims or exposes people to foreseeable danger. Warn readers before unavoidable disturbing imagery.
  • During emergencies, conflict, public-health events and disasters, prioritise verified safety information and identify the source and time of urgent instructions.
  • Do not publish operational details that could foreseeably endanger hostages, rescue operations or vulnerable people unless a clear public-interest assessment supports publication.

8. Images, video, audio and digital evidence

  • Verify the origin, date, location and context of user-submitted or third-party media when material to the story. Do not describe old or unrelated footage as current.
  • Do not materially manipulate documentary images or audio in a way that changes their meaning. Clearly label illustrations, reconstructions, composites, archive footage and materially edited media.
  • Preserve the original file and relevant metadata when possible. Explain meaningful uncertainty about authenticity.
  • Use photographs, music, video, graphics and text only when licensed, in the public domain, used with permission or otherwise lawfully available. Credit creators as required.

9. Artificial intelligence and automation

  • AI tools may assist research, transcription, translation, accessibility or editing, but do not replace human editorial responsibility.
  • Every material factual claim in AI-assisted journalism must be checked against reliable sources. AI-generated quotations, citations, events, sources or statistics must never be fabricated or published as real.
  • Clearly disclose AI-generated or synthetically altered images, audio or video when readers could reasonably mistake them for authentic documentary material.
  • Do not enter confidential source details, unpublished sensitive material or personal data into AI services unless the newsroom has approved the tool and the use is safe and lawful.

10. Sponsored content, advertising and affiliate links

  • Label advertising, sponsorship, affiliate links and paid partnerships clearly and conspicuously. Paid material must be distinguishable from independent editorial content on desktop and mobile.
  • Advertisers cannot dictate news coverage or suppress independent reporting. Sponsored claims should not be presented as independently verified facts without verification.
  • Reject advertisements that are deceptive, fraudulent, illegal, discriminatory, exploitative or otherwise inconsistent with applicable law and platform policy. Check claims and destination links proportionately to the risk.
  • Do not use deceptive ad placements, disguised download buttons, forced clicks or misleading “close” controls. Respect reader privacy and applicable rules for cookies, tracking and personal data.

11. User submissions, comments and community content

  • Identify user-generated material as such when necessary and make reasonable checks before republishing it as fact.
  • Do not knowingly publish threats, targeted harassment, hate, doxxing, impersonation, scams, unlawful content, non-consensual intimate imagery or content that exploits children.
  • Moderation should be consistent, proportionate and documented. Readers should have a way to report potentially harmful or inaccurate material.
  • Protect confidential sources and do not expose private correspondence or personal information without a lawful and justified reason.

12. Attribution, plagiarism and source protection

  • Credit other publications, researchers, photographers and creators when their work materially informs a story. Link to source material when practical.
  • Do not plagiarise, copy another outlet's reporting as original work, or remove attribution. Quotations and paraphrases must preserve meaning and be attributed appropriately.
  • Protect confidential sources, explain any promises of confidentiality to an editor, and never promise secrecy beyond what can responsibly be honoured.

13. Opinion, analysis and sensitive coverage

  • Label opinion, editorial, analysis, reviews and sponsored material clearly. Writers may argue a position, but factual claims still require evidence.
  • Cover elections, politics, religion, public health, crime and social conflict with careful sourcing, accurate terminology and scrutiny of claims from powerful actors.
  • Do not publish discriminatory incitement or knowingly amplify manipulated content without context. Report on harmful claims only when the public interest justifies it and the reporting adds verification or context.

14. Legal and ethical responsibilities

Contributors must respect applicable laws on defamation, privacy, contempt of court, election reporting, copyright, data protection, child protection and other relevant matters in the jurisdictions where reporting is produced and distributed. Legal compliance is a minimum requirement, not a substitute for ethical judgement. When serious legal or safety risks arise, escalate the matter to an editor and obtain qualified advice where appropriate.

15. Editorial review and enforcement

Editors are responsible for reviewing high-risk claims, serious allegations, sensitive personal information, anonymous sourcing, graphic material, manipulated media and paid partnerships before publication when practicable. Violations may lead to correction, removal, a published explanation, suspension of contributor access or other proportionate action. These standards should be reviewed periodically as law, technology and newsroom practice evolve.

16. Breaking news and live coverage

  • Move quickly without treating speed as permission to publish unverified claims. Clearly identify preliminary information and attribute it to its source.
  • Use timestamps and time zones where timing matters. Update or correct live blogs as the facts change and avoid leaving superseded instructions prominent.
  • Do not repeat casualty figures, arrest details, identity claims or emergency instructions from a single unconfirmed social post. Seek confirmation from responsible authorities and independent sources, while noting that official statements may also require scrutiny.
  • Do not publish rumours about the cause of a disaster, attack or public emergency before evidence supports them. Explain what remains unknown.

17. Elections, civic life and political reporting

  • Apply consistent standards to candidates, parties, public officials and civic groups. Scrutinise claims regardless of political affiliation.
  • Distinguish verified results, official projections, party claims, opinion polls and unverified tallies. Explain poll dates, sample limitations and margins of error when reporting polling.
  • Do not knowingly publish false voting dates, locations, eligibility rules, results or other information that could disenfranchise people. Verify procedural information against authoritative sources and date it.
  • Disclose political advertising and paid campaign material. News coverage must not be sold as independent reporting.

18. Crime, courts and allegations

  • Use precise legal language and distinguish suspicion, arrest, charge, trial, verdict, sentence, appeal and acquittal. An allegation is not a finding of guilt.
  • Verify court documents and identify the court, date and procedural stage when relevant. Do not imply that a charge or police statement is a judicial finding.
  • Consider the presumption of innocence, fair-trial rights, victim safety and the risk of identifying witnesses or vulnerable people. Avoid publishing details that could prejudice proceedings or expose sources to retaliation.
  • Correct reports when charges are dropped, a person is acquitted, or material proceedings change, and link to the earlier report where practical.

19. Health, science, climate and public-interest claims

  • Prefer peer-reviewed research, systematic reviews, recognised scientific bodies and qualified independent experts. Explain when evidence is preliminary, contested or limited.
  • Do not present anecdotes, testimonials, preprints or commercial claims as conclusive proof. Disclose relevant funding, conflicts and study limitations.
  • Give public-health guidance its source and date, and distinguish general information from individual medical advice. Do not promise cures or amplify dangerous misinformation without context.
  • For climate and environmental reporting, describe the strength of the evidence, relevant uncertainty and credible expert consensus accurately. Avoid false balance between well-supported findings and unsupported claims.

20. Financial, business and consumer reporting

  • Check figures against filings, audited statements, official data or clearly attributed sources. Explain units, currency, periods and whether figures are estimates or confirmed results.
  • Disclose ownership, investments, sponsorship, affiliate arrangements or other interests that could influence coverage. Separate reporting from investment advice and promotional claims.
  • Take special care with allegations of fraud, insolvency, misconduct or product danger. Give affected parties a fair opportunity to respond and explain the evidence behind the report.
  • Do not guarantee financial returns, endorse unverified investment schemes or direct readers to a product without appropriate verification and transparent disclosure.

21. Diversity, inclusion and respectful language

  • Use accurate names and descriptions, respect a person's stated identity where relevant, and avoid slurs except when strictly necessary for a clearly explained public-interest purpose.
  • Do not reduce a person or community to a stereotype. Seek a meaningful range of voices, especially from people directly affected by the issue being reported.
  • Explain cultural, religious and regional context without treating any community as monolithic. Avoid implying collective guilt for the actions of an individual.
  • Use accessible language, explain specialist terms, provide meaningful image alt text where possible and consider readers using mobile devices or assistive technology.

22. Source relationships, whistleblowers and security

  • Assess a source's access, motive, reliability and ability to know the facts. Corroborate claims and preserve records that support significant reporting.
  • Agree clearly on whether a conversation is on the record, on background or off the record before relying on it. Do not retroactively grant anonymity or confidentiality without editorial review.
  • Limit access to confidential source identities and sensitive documents. Use secure communication and storage proportionate to the risk, and avoid publishing metadata that could expose a source.
  • Never fabricate a source or promise absolute protection that the newsroom cannot guarantee. Escalate credible threats, legal demands and urgent safety concerns to the editor.

23. Copyright, licensing and attribution

  • Use original reporting and properly licensed material. A work being visible online does not automatically make it free to reproduce.
  • Credit photographers, agencies, archives, data providers and other creators according to licence terms and applicable law. Keep a record of permissions and licence restrictions.
  • Use quotations only to the extent justified by the reporting purpose and applicable law. Do not copy another outlet's article and present it as original work.
  • When readers submit images or documents, confirm who created them and whether publication permission and privacy considerations have been addressed.

24. Reader privacy, data and cybersecurity

  • Collect only reader information needed for a clear purpose. Explain relevant collection and use in an accessible privacy notice and follow applicable data-protection law.
  • Do not expose passwords, access tokens, private admin links, unpublished drafts, personal contact details or confidential newsroom records in public pages or source repositories.
  • Restrict administrative access by role, use strong unique passwords and multifactor authentication where available, and promptly remove access when it is no longer needed.
  • Use analytics and advertising technologies transparently, respect applicable consent requirements and do not claim that a feature is secure or private unless it has been assessed.

25. Complaints, appeals and editorial independence

  • Provide a clear route for readers to submit corrections, complaints and concerns. Acknowledge and assess substantive complaints promptly and impartially.
  • Keep a record of significant decisions, corrections and complaints, limiting access to personal data to people who need it for review.
  • Do not remove accurate reporting merely because a subject dislikes it. Equally, do not refuse a justified correction to protect reputation, commercial interests or a preferred narrative.
  • Editorial decisions should be made by responsible editors, with legal or specialist advice sought when necessary. Commercial staff and advertisers must not overrule independent editorial judgement.

26. Transparency, review and scope

  • Identify the author and publication or update date where practical. Explain material limitations, methods, corrections and use of anonymous or synthetic material when readers need that context.
  • Apply these standards to staff, freelancers, guest contributors, commissioned work, social posts, newsletters, video, audio, graphics, push alerts and all other channels operated by PARROTHIT.
  • Editors should review these standards at least annually and after significant changes in law, technology or newsroom practice. Staff and contributors should be told about material revisions.
  • These guidelines are an editorial framework, not legal advice or a guarantee of compliance with every jurisdiction. For cross-border reporting, consider the relevant laws, safety risks and ethical duties before publication.
